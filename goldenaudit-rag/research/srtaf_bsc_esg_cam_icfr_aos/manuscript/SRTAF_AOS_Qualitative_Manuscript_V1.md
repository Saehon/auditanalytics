# From Strategic Performance to Auditability: How Contractual and Sustainability Risks Travel across Management Control, ICFR and Critical Audit Matters

**Target journal:** *Accounting, Organizations and Society*  
**Manuscript:** Qualitative theory-building and documentary research, first draft V1.0  
**Prepared:** 20 November 2026  
**Principal investigator:** Saeid Homayoun, University of Gävle, Sweden  
**Status:** Working conceptual–methodological manuscript with a limited real-document feasibility pilot. NOT a completed 50-contract qualitative study, completed field study, or submission-ready findings paper.

## Abstract

How does an economic risk become a strategically consequential performance measure, an accounting estimate, a control concern, and ultimately a publicly auditable matter? Research on management control, sustainability measurement, internal control, and critical audit matters (CAMs) offers distinctive insights into each domain, but it offers less direct evidence about the organizational processes by which the same issue is transformed—or disappears—across these boundaries. We develop a qualitative research design and an emergent analytical framework, **Strategic Risk Translation and Assurance Feedback (SRTAF)**, for studying the movement of contractual and sustainability-related risks among balanced-scorecard (BSC) practices, environmental, social and governance (ESG) systems, financial reporting, internal control over financial reporting (ICFR), and auditor communication. We combine management-control theory, the sociology of accountability, auditability, and research on professional judgment and agentic information systems. The proposed empirical design begins with authenticated high-technology customer contracts, then traces selected risk mechanisms through management narratives and performance measures, accounting disclosures, control evidence, and CAMs; it explicitly preserves negative, indeterminate, and nonaligned trajectories. A feasibility assessment of three real SEC comment-response documents—one discussing IFRS 15 and two discussing US GAAP ASC 606—illustrates the difference between a regulatory query, management's account and independently established contractual or control evidence. No authentic high-technology customer contract has yet been validated for the intended 50-contract sample, and no CAM–ICFR issue match has been established. The paper therefore presents theoretical propositions, a source-grounded qualitative codebook, and a falsifiable process-tracing protocol rather than fabricated empirical findings. Its prospective contribution is to explain the selective production of strategic risk visibility and the professional boundaries of AI-mediated assurance.

**Keywords:** management control; balanced scorecard; sustainability; ESG; strategic risk; contractual uncertainty; IFRS 15; ASC 606; ICFR; critical audit matters; auditability; qualitative research; agentic AI.

## 1. Introduction

Organizations do not encounter risk as a single, stable object. An enterprise software contract may be commercially evaluated as a customer-retention opportunity, represented in a balanced scorecard as recurring revenue and service reliability, incorporated into a sustainability system as data privacy or energy-use exposure, translated into financial reporting as performance obligations and variable consideration, and presented to the auditor as an issue requiring judgment over revenue timing. At each stage, a different organizational actor, time horizon, evidentiary threshold and purpose governs what counts as relevant. The public audit report communicates only selected matters. Consequently, the absence of a CAM does not imply an absence of strategic risk, just as the existence of a CAM does not establish control failure.

This variation matters because management accounting and assurance research often examine partially separate representations. The balanced scorecard (BSC) draws managerial attention across financial, customer, internal-process, and learning-and-growth dimensions (Kaplan & Norton, 1992). Strategic performance measurement can develop organizational capabilities, but judgment and incentive systems can also reweight nonfinancial measures in practice (Henri, 2006; Lipe & Salterio, 2000; Ittner et al., 2003). Sustainability Balanced Scorecard scholarship proposes connections between business strategy and environmental and social management (Figge et al., 2002). More recently, Huber et al. (2025) document through a longitudinal qualitative case how enterprise risk management becomes integrated with an established BSC practice. Their research makes clear that BSC–ERM integration itself is not a sufficient novelty claim for a new study.

An organizational boundary problem remains: even when an economic concern attracts strategic attention, how—and under which conditions—does it become an accountable number, a control issue, or an audit matter? Roberts and Scapens (1985) characterize accounting practices through relationships of accountability. Power (1996) conceptualizes auditability as something actively constructed through institutional practices rather than automatically inherited from an underlying object. These perspectives invite the possibility that risk may be translated, displaced, condensed, or contested as it moves from commercial activity to distinct reporting and assurance regimes. Christensen et al. (2021) further show that sustainability reporting requirements have potential real and informational consequences, while also raising questions of incentives and implementation. A sustainability concern cannot, however, be presumed to enter ICFR: sustainability-information controls and ICFR often have different purposes and assurance scopes.

The reporting boundary is especially salient in the United States. Under PCAOB AS 3101, a CAM must meet specified audit-committee, material-account/disclosure and auditor-judgment criteria (PCAOB, n.d.). CAMs are neither a register of all enterprise risks nor a substitute for an ICFR opinion. Burke et al. (2023) examine the disclosure and consequences of U.S. CAMs, including changes to referenced footnotes. Dee et al. (2026) investigate the disciplining role of CAM communication in internal-control quality. These findings establish feedback between external reporting and organizational behavior as a plausible area of inquiry, but they do not demonstrate contract-specific continuity through BSC, ESG, ICFR and CAM systems. The unresolved issue is therefore not whether CAMs and ICFR are associated in general. It is how one economic concern becomes differentiated across distinct systems of calculation and accountability.

Developments in foundation-model and agentic-AI systems sharpen the puzzle. Retrieval systems may identify contractual cues, compare accounting rules and draft working-paper interpretations, but source retrieval does not certify the accounting implication of a contractual provision. Professional adjudication requires validated source versions, contractual context, complete evidence, a relevant financial-reporting framework and decision authority. Baird and Maruping (2021) theorize delegated activity between human actors and agentic information-systems artifacts. Experimental auditing research shows that reliance on AI advice may affect complex estimates (Commerford et al., 2022), while qualitative work documents the continuing and changing demands placed on auditors' judgment by digital technology (Samiolo et al., 2024). AI systems can thus be studied as contested participants in the production of organizational visibility, not as autonomous truth engines.

We develop **Strategic Risk Translation and Assurance Feedback (SRTAF)** as a *provisional, falsifiable, theory-building framework* for investigating these relationships. The central question is: **How, why and by whom do contractual and sustainability-related risks become visible, transformed, contested or absent as they move through BSC and ESG practices, financial-reporting controls, and auditor communication?** We focus on high-technology customer arrangements because software subscriptions, licenses, platform services, bundled maintenance, implementation and usage-based pricing create useful differences between commercial value drivers and technical revenue-accounting judgments. The framework distinguishes an IFRS 15 engagement from a US GAAP ASC 606 engagement; conceptual convergence does not make their legal and jurisdictional authority interchangeable (IFRS Foundation, n.d.).

This manuscript is a first draft with a bounded documentary feasibility pilot, not a claim of completed fieldwork. We offer three prospective contributions. First, we seek a management-accounting explanation of *risk visibility*: how scorecard metrics and sustainability indicators selectively make risks managerial objects. Second, we seek an organizational explanation of *documentary translation*: why issues are preserved, reframed, aggregated or rendered indeterminate when they enter accounts, controls, and assurance. Third, we examine *authorization and feedback*: how professional reviewers and AI-assisted systems shape which interpretations are contested or carried forward. The empirical warrant for these contributions is to be established through contract-first theoretical sampling, process tracing, human-reviewed qualitative coding, and deliberately retained negative cases.

## 2. Theoretical foundations

### 2.1 The BSC as an architecture of strategic attention rather than a complete risk register

Kaplan and Norton (1992) introduced four complementary scorecard perspectives designed to broaden performance assessment beyond financial measures. Yet the BSC does not automatically capture all relevant risks, and its categories cannot be treated as an objective map of a firm's entire risk universe. Performance measures are selected, contested and interpreted in organizational settings. Lipe and Salterio (2000) show that scorecard evaluation can privilege common over distinctive measures; Ittner et al. (2003) document how subjective weighting and managerial discretion can reshape what a scorecard rewards. Henri (2006) connects different uses of a management control system to capabilities supporting strategy. Together, these studies support our interpretation of the BSC as a **selective attention and mobilization infrastructure**.

For high-technology firms, a contract's consequences can span four perspectives. Financial measures may record recurring revenue, margins, cost-to-serve and contract profitability. Customer measures may reflect availability commitments, customer satisfaction, cancellations or data-security trust. Internal-process measures may reflect software implementation, system reliability, outsourced hosting and contract-data integration. Learning-and-growth measures may reflect product development capabilities, cybersecurity skills, training and model governance. These are sensitizing categories: a qualitative investigation should not infer actual BSC implementation from the existence of business KPIs in public disclosures.

### 2.2 ESG as a cross-cutting and politically consequential system of measurement

Figge et al. (2002) offer an early Sustainability Balanced Scorecard approach to strategic integration of social and environmental considerations. Sustainability reporting also develops its own reporting infrastructures, evidence rules and audience expectations. Christensen et al. (2021) explain why mandatory sustainability reporting could change informational arrangements and real activity, while emphasizing uncertainty over costs, incentives and behavioral responses. Consequently, ESG is best modeled here as a **cross-cutting interpretive and governance domain**, not as an automatically observed fifth scorecard perspective or a synonym for ICFR.

A cloud-software agreement illustrates the distinction. Energy-intensive hosting may matter for internal cost management and emissions disclosure; a privacy commitment may matter for customers and social or relationship capital; an outsourced data-centre agreement may matter for supplier controls. Whether any of these concerns also affects financial-statement recognition, measurement or disclosure depends on the actual contractual rights and obligations, reporting rules and materiality. Thus, SRTAF separates *ESG strategic salience*, *sustainability-reporting assertion*, *financial-statement assertion*, and *control operating effectiveness* as different constructs requiring different evidence.

### 2.3 Accountability, calculative translation and the production of auditability

Roberts and Scapens (1985) treat accounting as embedded in organizational accountability relations. Power (1996) argues that auditability is achieved through knowledge and institutional practices that make objects amenable to verification. These arguments shift attention from an underlying risk's presumed objective severity to the practices through which it becomes defined, measured and defensible. The same software contract may be described as a customer experience commitment by sales managers, a cost or service-delivery forecast by the controller, a revenue-recognition judgment by financial reporting, and an evidence-sufficiency question by an auditor. The differences are not necessarily failures: each setting may have a legitimate scope and decision criterion.

SRTAF refers to this movement as **strategic risk translation**. We conceptualize six possible documentary states: preservation of the underlying issue; transformation into a different category; aggregation with other risks; explicit contradiction; remediation or reclassification over time; and absent/indeterminate representation. A critical distinction is between *verified absence* and *no available evidence of presence*. Public reports alone rarely establish the whole internal decision pathway. A missing CAM or sustainability disclosure is therefore not coded as proof of no relevant risk; it is coded by the reporting regime's applicability, reviewed source coverage, and degree of issue-level confirmation.

### 2.4 ICFR, CAMs and the boundaries of assurance

Financial controls are designed to support reliable financial reporting. Control failures can arise from contract-management processes, revenue-system interfaces, journal processing, estimates or inadequate management review, but financial-reporting complexity is not itself a control deficiency. CAMs concern matters that, within the PCAOB-defined audit setting, meet the specific criteria for communication in an auditor's report. Under AS 3101 a CAM does not automatically indicate a material weakness (PCAOB, n.d.). Burke et al. (2023) and Dee et al. (2026) provide evidence of disclosure and internal-control consequences associated with CAM reporting, establishing a strong context for examining potential feedback without assuming an automatic one-to-one link.

For qualitative work, a **contract–accounting–ICFR–CAM issue match** requires more than shared words such as *revenue*, *license* or *estimate*. The original contract clause, relevant accounting note, control process, same issuer and fiscal-year chronology, and complete auditor CAM passage must support the proposed connection. Negative cases include identical contract types yielding different reporting trajectories and CAMs with no verified connection to the selected contract. US CAMs and ISA 701 Key Audit Matters (KAMs) are institutionally distinct and should not be pooled without separate coding.

### 2.5 AI-mediated evidence and nondelegable professional responsibility

Baird and Maruping (2021) theorize delegation to and from agentic information-systems artifacts. This is useful for understanding how a researcher, accountant or auditor could instruct specialized agents to retrieve documents, surface counterevidence and prepare interpretations. Yet delegation can relocate attention and responsibility in ways that are hard to observe. Commerford et al. (2022) examine auditor reliance on AI for complex estimates. Based on qualitative interviews, Samiolo et al. (2024) argue that technologically mediated audit work creates new demands for judgment rather than simply replacing low-level repetitive work. SRTAF therefore treats an AI-generated link between BSC, ESG, IFRS 15, ICFR and CAM as a *candidate interpretation* until authentic documents and authorized human reviewers support it.

We distinguish four technical functions: retrieval and extraction, candidate risk classification, independent evidence challenge, and human adjudication. The system's Standards-as-Code layer should keep date, jurisdiction, evidence scope and obligation type explicit. Its outputs are not audit opinions. A model's fluent narrative is not a substitute for underlying operational records or professional sign-off. This distinction is theoretically relevant: it exposes when *technical legibility* is mistaken for *organizational or professional auditability*.

## 3. Strategic Risk Translation and Assurance Feedback (SRTAF)

SRTAF is a proposed middle-range **process framework**, not an empirically certified causal law. Its analytical sequence begins with contractually or sustainability-grounded concerns, examines their entry into BSC/ESG practices, traces their translation into financial accounting and controls, investigates possible visibility in CAM reporting, and follows any feedback to strategic attention and managerial practice. Each transition is optional and empirically contingent. Organizations can route issues through parallel reporting tracks; a matter may remain strategically significant but never qualify for CAM communication. Some issues are genuinely resolved before they reach public reporting, while others remain opaque because of information restrictions or scope differences.

**Transition T1: Recognition.** A contractual or ESG concern gains an identifiable owner and enters performance or risk discussions. Evidence: contemporaneous scorecards, risk logs, committee minutes and management interviews. Rival interpretation: the issue was salient independently of BSC practices.

**Transition T2: Calculation.** Managers translate the concern into measures such as margins, customer churn, carbon intensity, implementation cost, expected rebates or delivery milestones. Evidence: metric definitions, cost models and revised forecasts. Rival interpretation: a metric is displayed but does not influence decisions.

**Transition T3: Financial-accounting classification.** If relevant, the issue becomes a financial reporting assertion (for example, the number or nature of performance obligations, transaction price, allocation, recognition timing or disclosure). Evidence: signed contracts, legal interpretations, accounting memoranda and original financial statements. Rival interpretation: a commercial KPI has no financial-statement consequence.

**Transition T4: Control verification.** Management and reviewers establish whether relevant design and operating-effectiveness controls exist and whether testable evidence is sufficient. Evidence: process narratives, data lineage, control populations, exception records, remediation and authorized sign-off. Rival interpretation: complex judgment with no control weakness.

**Transition T5: Assurance narration.** External auditors may communicate an issue as a CAM when the institutional criteria are met. Evidence: full audit opinion, relevant audit committee communications if authorized, fiscal-year eligibility, and source-grounded issue matching. Rival interpretation: a risk is addressed but not CAM-eligible; public silence is not a negative finding.

**Transition T6: Feedback and repair.** Review, challenge or public scrutiny may affect future scorecards, ESG measures, policies and allocations. Evidence: dated before/after documents and participant explanations. Rival interpretation: exogenous market or regulatory changes, not assurance feedback, caused the adjustment.

### 3.1 Qualitative propositions

**P1 (strategic selection):** A risk's visibility in management discussions depends partly on the scorecard's measures, incentives and strategic uses; risks with weak metric ownership may remain narratively peripheral even when economically relevant (Henri, 2006; Lipe & Salterio, 2000; Ittner et al., 2003).

**P2 (boundary transformation):** Contractual and ESG issues are more likely to be reclassified than preserved verbatim when entering financial reporting or control evaluation, because each regime has different accountability and evidentiary requirements (Roberts & Scapens, 1985; Power, 1996; Christensen et al., 2021).

**P3 (selective auditability):** CAM communication and ICFR conclusions will exhibit systematic, sometimes legitimate, discontinuities from BSC/ESG concerns; neither CAM absence nor complex contractual judgment establishes effective or ineffective controls (PCAOB, n.d.; Burke et al., 2023; Dee et al., 2026).

**P4 (feedback and repair):** Where assurance concerns are mobilized by responsible actors, subsequent documentation may show changes in risk monitoring, budgeting, contract administration or disclosure; temporal association alone is insufficient to attribute causation (Huber et al., 2025; Dee et al., 2026).

**P5 (AI-mediated translation):** Evidence-retrieval and specialist-agent configurations may change which possible linkages become visible and how quickly they are reviewed, while also introducing fabricated links, source contamination and accountability ambiguities (Baird & Maruping, 2021; Commerford et al., 2022; Samiolo et al., 2024).

These propositions guide abductive case comparison; they are *not hypotheses confirmed by the present pilot*. We anticipate revising or rejecting them when incompatible evidence emerges.

## 4. Research design: qualitative, comparative and contract-first

### 4.1 Interpretive stance and case boundaries

The research takes an abductive, process-oriented qualitative approach. Ahrens and Chapman (2006) provide guidance on positioning management-accounting field material to contribute to theory rather than treating pre-coded variables as substitutes for situated practice. Huber et al. (2025) offer a recent longitudinal BSC–ERM example, while Samiolo et al. (2024) demonstrate the value of qualitative investigation into how auditors negotiate technological change. The object of explanation is the **risk-translation episode**: an identifiable contractual or sustainability-related concern, its changing representations and the actors and evidence supporting or disputing each transformation.

Case boundaries should include an issuer, a contractual issue, the relevant period, its strategic/managerial treatment where observable, relevant accounting disclosures, and any documented controls or audit narration. The relevant primary unit is an *issue trajectory* rather than a contract document or firm-year alone. Embedded units include clauses, performance measures, sustainability indicators, control statements, reporting notes, CAM paragraphs, and interview passages. The order of analysis must remain contract-first to avoid selecting only issues already appearing in CAMs or reported weaknesses.

### 4.2 Sources and selection

The initial *target*, not accomplished sample, is 50 distinct authenticated high-technology customer contracts, with software/SaaS, IP licence, support/maintenance, reseller/distribution and cloud-service arrangements providing purposive variation. CUAD offers publicly filed agreement material and clause annotations, but legal labels are not IFRS 15 judgments. CUAD's source coverage and licence should be verified for the selected files; critical clauses must be traced back to original SEC exhibits. A SEC Exhibit 10 filing is not the same document as a later 10-K or SEC staff correspondence. Only actual source bytes with provenance, filing dates and agreement identifiers qualify for contract-level coding.

The 50-contract screening frame will be reduced through theoretical sampling to an illustrative target of 12–20 intensive issue trajectories, varied by risk mechanism, disclosure trajectory, reporting framework, observed control evidence, organizational setting and negative-case status. These are design targets, not achieved counts or saturation claims. The research may expand to organizations outside SEC filings only if authorized internal source documents and participant access exist. Multiple commercial agreements involving the same firm cannot be treated as independent issuer-years without an explicit multilevel design.

**Source hierarchy:** (1) signed or filed contract and amendments; (2) contemporaneous revenue accounting note and policy; (3) authenticated management-control, BSC and ESG materials (if accessible); (4) ICFR assessments and internal-control evidence; (5) full external audit report and CAM or KAM where in scope; (6) follow-up disclosure, remediation and participant testimony. For each stage record issuer, CIK or other identifier, fiscal year, original filing accession, exact locator, source version, text hash, author/actor, rights status and contradiction status. Do not treat a firm's promotional ESG webpage as independently verified sustainability performance.

### 4.3 IFRS 15 / ASC 606 accounting focus

An accounting specialist first verifies the applicable reporting framework, entity, period and contractual role. The research then uses the five-step revenue model as an *investigative ordering device*: identify the customer contract; identify performance obligations; determine transaction price; allocate consideration; and determine when obligations are satisfied (IFRS Foundation, n.d.). Candidate mechanisms include distinct software versus support services, right-to-use versus right-to-access intellectual property, usage-based or variable pricing, customer acceptance, modification, refunds, renewal rights and principal–agent assessment. Each code must be anchored to the actual wording of the agreement, not merely regulatory commentary. IFRS 15 and ASC 606 share a converged history, but conclusions cannot be labeled IFRS-compliant for a US GAAP filer without appropriate analysis.

### 4.4 BSC, ESG and control-layer evidence

The first qualitative pass asks whether strategic actors identify an issue; the second asks how it enters decisions and measures; the third asks whether a distinct reporting or control consequence can be documented. BSC ownership is verified only through authentic artifacts (scorecards, strategic plans, review meeting notes, budgets) or credible interviews. BSC categories can be used as a researcher-defined sensitizing framework even where an actual BSC system is absent, but the results must label this difference. ESG integration requires evidence of an identified environmental, social or governance issue and the organizational practices attached to it. ICFR matters must be distinguished from controls over sustainability disclosures. Control design and operating effectiveness are distinct: a disclosed control policy is not proof it operated.

### 4.5 Coding, triangulation and trustworthiness

A two-stage coding approach combines theory-informed codes (R1–R8, BSC domains, ESG themes, and documentary translation states) with inductively developed codes. Each quotation-level record will contain: case ID, source hash, exact span and page/section, risk mechanism, BSC domain, ESG status, actor/voice, date, reporting regime, accounting issue, claim-versus-evidence classification, inferred linkage, rival interpretation, reviewer status and adjudication decision. A separate qualitative memo will explain why related documents do or do not concern the same underlying issue.

At least two researchers should independently code a bounded calibration subset (provisionally 10 of the eventual 50 contracts, depending on access) before resolving disagreements with a documented adjudication protocol. The primary objective is interpretive credibility rather than an artificial reliability coefficient; agreement measures can supplement, not replace, contextual review when category definitions are sufficiently stable. A third reviewer should challenge negative cases and seemingly self-evident matches. Participant/member validation is desirable where access permits, subject to confidentiality and the risk of strategic self-presentation. Saturation is not claimed until theoretical variation and negative-case inquiry are demonstrated.

### 4.6 Longitudinal process tracing and rival explanations

For each intensive case, build an event chronology with contract effective date, filing date, management control review, reporting period, financial statement disclosure, control evaluation, auditor report date, and later remediation or disclosure changes. A proposed T1–T6 transition is supported only where source dates and process evidence permit the sequence. Rival explanations include industry pressure, commercial renegotiation, accounting-rule changes, organizational restructuring, auditor selection effects, and different materiality scopes. Especially important is the **negative linkage**: a clear customer-data risk may be present in a contract but absent from revenue accounting because it has no recognition or measurement consequence. That is not necessarily a reporting failure. Conversely, a CAM concerning revenue estimates may cover an issuer's portfolio without being traceable to any sampled contract.

### 4.7 Agentic-AI research instrument and authorization controls

GoldenAudit's Standards-as-Code prototype is used as a design and candidate-screening instrument, not as an autonomous auditor. Separate agents may be configured for (a) contract retrieval and clause spans; (b) technical revenue-accounting questions; (c) BSC/ESG conceptual mapping; (d) ICFR and CAM source discovery; (e) skeptical counterevidence; and (f) human approval. Agent names alone do not prove distinct model executions. Pre-registered tests will compare keyword screening and single-agent extraction against an evidence-verified multi-agent workflow only after authenticated, rights-cleared source text and human-reviewed reference labels exist. Log retrieval configuration, versioned source hashes, exact prompt/model identity, latency, invalid evidence links, abstentions and reviewer overrides. External-AI processing of confidential records requires institutional and data-owner authorization.

### 4.8 Research ethics, originality and replicability

The project must not release proprietary customer contracts, private scorecards, licensed standards clauses, identifiable employees, confidential control tests or sealed benchmark labels through public GitHub. Original SEC public exhibits may be linked with accession and evidence locators, subject to applicable terms; any mirrored training corpus requires a fresh rights audit. The public replication package should provide the research protocol, nonconfidential case register, codebook, qualitative decision log, source URLs, text hashes where permissible, analysis scripts, redaction log, and reproduced public summary tables. The original data and code of other studies must not be represented as this project's empirical output. This manuscript differs from the existing contract–CAM–ICFR documentary project: here the substantive theory concerns **strategic measurement, sustainability attention and organizational translation**, not merely contract-to-audit documentary continuity.

## 5. Bounded documentary feasibility pilot: observations and limits

The prior GoldenAudit IFRS 15 high-technology pilot retrieved and provisionally coded three authentic SEC staff-comment response files from the investigator's Google Drive archive. **Lightspeed Commerce** (2022 correspondence) contained discussion of software subscriptions, subscription licences, the distinction between service and intellectual-property promises, and a principal-versus-agent issue explicitly framed under IFRS 15. **Cloudera** (2020 correspondence) contained discussion of proprietary software licences and support as distinct obligations, revenue recognition under US GAAP ASC 606, and a specific SEC staff question about management's ICFR-effectiveness disclosure. **National Instruments** (2019 correspondence) contained discussion of stand-alone selling prices for bundled software licences and maintenance under US GAAP. These observations are derived from regulatory correspondence and management explanations, not from signed customer agreements.

The earlier documentary pilot recorded ten provisional interpretive units across the three files. This number describes the earlier preliminary codebook exercise and does not indicate ten independent customer contracts or mutually exclusive risk events. The subsequent deterministic word-count check identified explicit accounting references in each source, but counting phrases cannot validate contract-level IFRS or ASC judgments. None of the three correspondence files supplied a verified contract–CAM–ICFR issue-level link. The Cloudera staff query regarding ICFR disclosure is a question about the wording of management's report; it is not itself a verified material weakness. These limitations reveal why SRTAF requires separate levels of data and inferential claims.

The feasibility pilot yields **one methodological insight**, not a theory confirmation: reporting discourse can identify a plausible accounting issue before the underlying contract, managerial scorecard, sustainability information and actual control evidence are visible. Thus a well-specified qualitative protocol must trace *evidence boundaries* as carefully as it traces thematic resemblance. It remains an open empirical question whether subsequent document collection reveals preserved connections, genuine discontinuity, or no connection between the issue and the BSC/ESG/CAM pathway. Claims regarding organizational causation, control effectiveness, commercial consequences or model superiority cannot be made at this stage.

## 6. Anticipated interpretive patterns and competing explanations (not findings)

The fieldwork will investigate at least four competing configurations. **Integrated translation** arises where a contract-related risk is demonstrably identified in management discussions, allocated to owners and performance measures, translated into an accounting or sustainability assertion, and supported by control evidence. **Selective decoupling** arises where risks appear in scorecards or ESG documents but not in financial reporting, perhaps because scopes and recognition rules differ legitimately. **Retrospective repair** arises when review or external scrutiny is followed by measurable process revisions, with chronology and rival causes examined. **Symbolic integration** arises where a polished dashboard or AI-generated evidence chain produces the appearance of control integration without supporting actions or data.

These are sensitizing analytic possibilities. Research should actively seek cases that contradict the proposed ordering, including organizations with effective financial controls but poor ESG documentation, prominent CAMs without related disclosed material weaknesses, valuable intangible capital with no validated accounting measurement effect, and a management-risk issue addressed without formal BSC use. If credible cases consistently show that professional and organizational translations proceed in parallel rather than sequentially, the model must be revised. An AOS contribution would arise from explaining why boundaries operate in specific ways, not from confirming every proposed linkage.

## 7. Discussion: proposed contributions to management accounting and assurance research

### 7.1 Management control as a selective risk-visibility system

The research can extend strategic performance measurement beyond the question of whether organizations use financial and nonfinancial indicators. It asks how a risk's entry into scorecard discussions affects its capacity to be owned, budgeted, acted upon and subsequently represented in other systems. This bridges Henri (2006), Ittner et al. (2003), and the longitudinal BSC–ERM integration study of Huber et al. (2025). The argument avoids claiming that BSC adoption automatically improves risk management. Instead, it examines the organizational construction of attention and the conditions under which measurement creates a durable chain of accountability.

### 7.2 Sustainability and financial reporting as distinct but interacting accountability regimes

The ESG layer brings into view issues that conventional contract profitability or ICFR analysis may not capture, such as energy intensity, stakeholder trust, employee capabilities and supplier practices. Figge et al. (2002) and Christensen et al. (2021) motivate the organizational relevance of such measurement. Yet the framework emphasizes that sustainability disclosures and financial statements follow different evidentiary rules. The empirical question is how firms negotiate these boundaries and what kinds of corroboration are considered sufficient by management, assurance practitioners and external stakeholders. This provides a basis for studying substantive integration without collapsing all ESG variables into unvalidated numerical risk scores.

### 7.3 Auditability as a negotiated endpoint, not an inevitable disclosure

The sociological insight from Roberts and Scapens (1985) and Power (1996) is that the passage from economic commitments to auditable objects is institutionally mediated. Audit reports cannot be interpreted as universal risk registers. Research on CAMs and internal controls provides an important boundary of novelty (Burke et al., 2023; Dee et al., 2026): the paper's contribution is not merely a statistical association between CAM disclosures and control outcomes. Rather, it is a contextual explanation of why the *same specific issue* may be rendered differently as management-control evidence, financial reporting evidence, a control concern and external audit narration. The study will distinguish appropriate professional silence from opaque or misleading omission through documentary scope and practitioner evidence.

### 7.4 Agentic AI and the politics of evidentiary legibility

The contribution of AI is to expose how an information system can influence which representations appear credible and connected. When retrieval systems generate fluent but unsupported links, they may produce **synthetic auditability**: a documentable-looking pathway with insufficient contractual or professional proof. When evidence passports, contradiction checks and human authorization are enforced, AI may instead support transparent contestation. This interpretation builds on delegated agentic artifacts (Baird & Maruping, 2021), reliance on AI advice in auditing (Commerford et al., 2022), and the situated demands of digital-audit judgment (Samiolo et al., 2024). These ideas are theoretical expectations pending observed agent use, logs and professional interviews.

## 8. Limitations, study gates and conclusion

This first draft has five limitations. First, the current three-document source base is SEC regulatory correspondence, not a sample of authenticated revenue contracts. Second, organizational BSC and ESG practice has not yet been observed. Third, CAM and ICFR issue-level linkages have not been verified. Fourth, qualitative coding has not undergone independent double-coder assessment or interview triangulation. Fifth, actual foundation-model experimental arms have not been run. As a result, the material in Sections 6 and 7 is a theoretical research agenda, not empirical results, and should not be submitted as a completed findings paper.

The next defensible milestone is a **50-contract provenance-locked screening and 10-case source triangulation gate**. For each contract, verify SEC accession and text, issue period, reporting framework and revenue-accounting mechanism; obtain same-issuer financial disclosures and, where possible, relevant organizational-management evidence. Only then conduct CAM/ICFR issue tracing and BSC/ESG comparative analysis. Qualitative interviews with controllers, risk managers, sustainability officers, internal auditors and external-audit professionals should address causal pathways and rival accounts, subject to organizational access and ethics approval. Complete at least one independently reviewed calibration round before writing claims about recurrent mechanisms.

SRTAF offers a grounded conceptual starting point for investigating why strategic and sustainability-related concerns do not move unchanged across accounting and assurance systems. Its value lies in preserving the distinctive purposes of performance measurement, financial reporting, internal control, ESG governance and external audit communication while tracing the organizational work required to connect them. Its strongest eventual AOS contribution will be a process explanation of selective risk visibility and professional accountability, supported by within-case evidence and negative cases rather than a claim that integration is always desirable or that AI has already achieved professional judgment.
